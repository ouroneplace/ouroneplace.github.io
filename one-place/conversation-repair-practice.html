Conversation Repair Practice

A small misunderstanding can grow when we guess what the other person means. Practice three moves: notice what is known, listen to understand, and offer a clear next step.

Use this for ordinary disagreements, not unsafe situations. You do not owe anyone a conversation, forgiveness or continued contact. If there are threats, coercion, intimidation or abuse, put safety and trusted support first. Local help and laws vary worldwide.

MOVE 1 OF 3 · NOTICE

What happened—and what is only a guess?

Fictional situation

Two housemates, Kai and Rowan, agreed to tidy the kitchen before dinner. Kai says: “I thought the counters would be clear before we cooked. They're still covered. Can we sort this out?” You are Rowan.

Why this matters: A visible mess and a request to talk are observable. A belief about Kai's intentions is an interpretation. Check the story before reacting to it.

Which statement stays closest to the evidence?

Read an example explanation

We know the counter was covered and Kai asked to talk. We do not yet know what Kai thinks about Rowan overall, or why Kai spoke that way. Ask rather than invent a motive.

MOVE 2 OF 3 · LISTEN

Check what you heard

Kai continues

“I needed room to prepare food. I felt frustrated, and I'm not sure we understood the plan the same way.”

How to try it: Reflect a concrete need and ask if you understood. Listening does not require agreeing with every accusation or accepting a task you cannot do.

What might Rowan say next?

Read an example explanation

“It sounds like you needed room to cook. Did I get that right?” checks your understanding. A specific pause can also be appropriate if you cannot listen well right now. Neither requires guessing motives or surrendering your limits.

MOVE 3 OF 3 · CHOOSE

Make a request without promising too much

Rowan's perspective

You believed you agreed to wash the dishes, not to clear all the counters. Both people may have understood the plan differently.

Check the result: Your next step names the difference, makes a realistic offer or limit, and leaves room for the other person to say no or suggest an alternative.

Which response would you want to explore?

Read an example explanation

A clear offer and a question create room for agreement; a respectful limit and a suggested later time can work too. Overpromising, demanding agreement, or forcing a repair may make the situation worse.

TRANSFER TO REAL LIFE · OPTIONAL

Put the three moves into your own words

Before using anything in a real conversation, adapt it to the relationship, culture, language, accessibility needs, and safety of the situation. This is an example, not a script you must follow.

  1. State one observation without assigning motives.
  2. Check what the other person needed or understood.
  3. Offer one realistic action, request, boundary, or pause.

Reflections completed: 0 of 3. You can explore any choice; there is no score.

Read a complete example message

Complete sample message: “I see that the counter wasn't clear when you needed it. I thought I agreed to do dishes, so we may have understood the plan differently. Could you tell me what you expected? I can do the dishes after dinner. Could we decide who clears the counter before cooking next time?”

Remember: A respectful response cannot guarantee a good outcome. If the other person refuses a conversation, you can respect that and choose your next safe action. If you made a specific mistake, acknowledge it without making excuses; if the arrangement was unclear, clarify without automatically taking all the blame.
