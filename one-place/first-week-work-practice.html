First Week at Work Practice

Starting a job brings new people, instructions, schedules, and expectations. Practice three everyday conversations before you need them. Learn what to say, what to check, and when to get other help.

Read first: These are fictional learning examples, not employer policies, legal advice, or a reporting service. Your workplace, contract, union agreement, and local law may differ. No names, employer records, medical details, or personal work hours are requested or saved. If a situation is immediately dangerous, move to safety and contact appropriate emergency or workplace safety help; do not rely on a practice exercise.

A simple four-move method

1 · Notice

What is unclear, missing, or not working? Separate what happened from what you assume.

2 · Ask

Make one respectful, specific request. You can ask for a written example, a point of contact, or a usable format.

3 · Check

Repeat the next step in your own words and confirm the right place to find the official record or instructions.

4 · Follow through: Complete the safe action, keep accurate information using workplace-approved methods, and ask again when something changes. Never guess through an immediate safety risk.

Three first-week situations

Each example has a plain-language guide and an optional multiple-choice practice. When scripting is unavailable, every guide and model answer remains readable. These situations do not ask for your own work details.

1 · An assignment is unclear

Fictional situation: A new teammate is asked to “finish the closeout” but has not been shown which checklist or deadline applies.

WHY: A specific question prevents avoidable mistakes. HOW: Ask which checklist to use, which step is first, and who will check completion.

CHECK: Confirm the deadline in your own words. COMMON MISTAKE: Pretending to understand because you want to look capable. STOP: If a task involves unfamiliar equipment or a hazard, ask for instruction before attempting it.

Read the model response without taking the quiz

“I want to complete this correctly. Which closeout checklist should I use, what comes first, and when should I check back?”

Which response is the clearest first step?

2 · Your recorded hours look different

Fictional situation: A training shift and the hours shown in a timekeeping system do not seem to match.

WHY: Accurate records matter. HOW: Compare the shift you actually worked with the official time record and ask which correction process applies.

CHECK: Note the date and verify whether the record was corrected. COMMON MISTAKE: Changing an official entry without checking the process or assuming a pay rule applies everywhere. STOP: If you suspect a rights violation or pressure to falsify hours, use a trusted official labor resource or qualified adviser.

Read the model response without taking the quiz

“My record for this fictional training shift looks different from the hours I worked. Where can I review it, and how do I request a correction?”

What is the safest useful first step?

3 · Instructions are not usable

Fictional situation: New-worker instructions are spoken quickly in a noisy room, and a teammate cannot follow every step.

WHY: Asking for a usable explanation is better than hiding a barrier. HOW: Request a repeat, written steps, or a different communication format. Some work-related accommodation requests may have legal protections in the U.S.; individual rules and documentation depend on circumstances.

CHECK: Confirm the key steps and safe contact for further questions. COMMON MISTAKE: Assuming everyone learns the same way or demanding a coworker's medical explanation. STOP: Do not attempt safety-critical tasks without understanding required training; seek an appropriate supervisor, accessibility contact, or safety resource.

Read the model response without taking the quiz

“Could we review those steps again or use a written checklist? I want to make sure I understand the task before doing it.”

Which next step respects the worker and the task?
