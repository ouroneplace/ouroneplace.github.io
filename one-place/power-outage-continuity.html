Power Outage Continuity Check

Plan how your household will stay informed, protect essential needs, and know when to leave or call for help during a power outage.

This is for households of different sizes and resources, including people who use powered medical equipment, mobility or communication aids, caregivers, children, older adults, people with temporary limitations, and pets or service animals.

Emergency happening now? This page does not monitor outages, weather, shelters, or medical devices. For immediate danger, contact your local emergency number (911 in the United States). Follow current official local instructions about evacuation and sheltering. Never delay urgent care to fill in a worksheet.

Non-negotiable safety: Never run a generator, grill, camping stove or engine indoors, in a garage, or close to openings. For portable generators, U.S. CDC guidance says outdoors more than 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, with battery-backed carbon monoxide alarms. Never use a gas oven to heat a home. Do not handle downed wires or flooded electrical equipment.

2. Learn what to do and what to avoid

Start with ordinary preparation before a storm or outage. If an emergency is already underway, use local instructions and the relevant stop rule.

Powered devices and mobility support

Why it matters
An outage can interrupt powered medical equipment, powered mobility, or communication and alert devices.
Before
Ask the equipment supplier or clinical team which backup power methods are approved, how long they last, and what to do if power fails. Check charging and suitable transport or alternate care options ahead of time.
During
Use only an approved and tested plan. If necessary support cannot continue safely, contact the designated clinical/emergency service promptly; do not improvise wiring or medical-device power.
After
Follow manufacturer/clinical instructions before reusing devices that were wet, damaged, overheated or affected by unstable power.
Common mistake
Assuming a utility medical-priority registry guarantees uninterrupted power or immediate rescue.
Check or stop
Can the user and designated helper describe a verified backup and escalation route? If not, contact the responsible provider before an outage; seek urgent help if vital support has failed.

Medicine and cold storage continuity

Why it matters
Some medicines and supplies need temperature control; their limits depend on the individual product.
Before
Ask a pharmacist or clinician about emergency refills, the manufacturer's storage limits, and a safe cooler or backup storage plan. Keep a private medication list outside this website.
During
Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed when practical. Follow the product's storage directions; do not guess medicine stability from the temperature of ordinary food.
After
If required temperatures were missed, contact the pharmacist, clinician or manufacturer about whether an item is usable or needs replacement.
Common mistake
Discarding all essential medicine automatically or assuming any warmed refrigerated medicine is safe.
Check or stop
Do you know whom to call for product-specific guidance? If life-sustaining medicine may be unavailable, seek urgent professional advice; do not independently stop or change treatment.

Alerts, communication and support

Why it matters
Power and network outages can silence a phone, hearing device, internet connection, or spoken-only warning.
Before
Identify official local alert channels in more than one usable format; charge devices or have an appropriate backup. Agree on paper contacts, a backup contact method and a check-in arrangement.
During
Use an available phone, text, radio, paper plan or agreed check-in method. Do not assume one technology or a single caregiver will be reachable.
After
Reconnect with people who rely on you, update any contact plan that failed and check communication-device batteries.
Common mistake
Using only an app, a voice-only alert, or a device that needs the power you just lost.
Check or stop
Can every household member receive vital information in a format they can use? If someone cannot be contacted and is in danger, follow local emergency guidance.

Safe location and accessible evacuation

Why it matters
Extreme temperatures, unsafe stairs or loss of a lift can make staying home or leaving unsafe.
Before
Identify a local destination and alternate destination, how to get there, needed mobility support, and how you will confirm access and transportation. Ask everyone what support they want.
During
Follow current official evacuation or shelter instructions. If someone cannot safely leave or stay, call the locally responsible emergency service; do not force an unsafe transfer.
After
Check hazards and official advice before returning to a damaged building. Never touch downed lines or wet electrical equipment.
Common mistake
Assuming every shelter is accessible, that an elevator will work, or that a voluntary assistance registry guarantees rescue.
Check or stop
Has the actual destination or service confirmed the accommodation? If not, plan an alternative and seek help without promising access.

Pets, service animals and dependents

Why it matters
Animals and people who depend on others need supplies, transport and a workable check-in plan.
Before
Identify food, water, essential supplies, suitable carriers or mobility support, and at least one appropriate pet/service-animal destination. Include children or other dependents without assuming a single helper.
During
Use a safe, prearranged plan and current local evacuation information. Do not enter unsafe areas or delay necessary human emergency response to retrieve belongings.
After
Check well-being and supplies, and confirm that the return environment is safe for people and animals.
Common mistake
Assuming every temporary shelter accepts pets or that service-animal rules are identical worldwide.
Check or stop
Can you confirm a suitable local option? If not, contact the relevant local provider ahead of time; immediate risk needs emergency assistance.

3. Practice one decision

Fictional situation: The lights go out. Someone suggests running a gasoline-powered generator inside an open garage to keep a refrigerator working.

What should happen first?

Choose one option or open the explanation.

Read the explanation without the activity

Never run fuel-burning generators or grills indoors, even with a garage door open. Outdoor generators must be placed safely away from openings (U.S. CDC recommends more than 20 feet) and used with working CO alarms; follow manufacturer and local requirements. In an active CO alarm or suspected poisoning, leave for fresh air and call local emergency services.

4. Paper checklist — works offline

Write these on paper with a person who wants to participate. Keep actual names, telephone numbers, medicine lists and locations privately with the household, not on One Place.

  1. Which official alerts can we receive without home power, including accessible formats?
  2. Who depends on electricity, medicines or accessible equipment, and what has a professional approved as a backup?
  3. Which safe lighting, temperature and carbon-monoxide protections are ready?
  4. How will we check on one another if phones or internet fail?
  5. Where could we go, how would we travel, and is it accessible?
  6. What will children, pets and service animals need?
  7. What is the precise situation that means we stop and call qualified or emergency help?

Success check: Everyone can identify one usable alert method, one safe next action, and a reliable way to get help. If a critical question is unresolved, write “need to confirm”—do not guess.
