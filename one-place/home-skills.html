Homeowners can do many ordinary tasks themselves, but gas lines, live electrical wiring, structural damage, roof work, garage-door springs, major sewage or mold problems, and suspect lead/asbestos may require trained professionals. Start with a few things every household can learn

Know how to spot a water leak, test smoke and CO alarms, use an HVAC filter correctly, clean without mixing chemicals, and recognize an emergency. Then build up to simple repairs.

DIY doesn't mean going it alone. A wise homeowner knows the difference between routine care, a repair that requires extra caution, problems you should only inspect, and jobs that need a qualified specialist.

Start here: know your home and basic tools ＋

Before choosing a job: Find your equipment's brand and model number, read its official manual, identify hazards and permissions, and decide whether you can safely shut off the relevant supply. A general lesson does not override the model manual or local requirements.

  • Flashlight: See accessible leaks, labels and loose parts without reaching into equipment.
  • Camera and notebook: Record the model number, starting condition, and what changed. Avoid posting personal information.
  • Measuring tape: Check dimensions before obtaining replacement strips, filters or parts.
  • Cloth and soft brush: Clean compatible surfaces without abrasive damage; check the product label.
  • Correct screwdriver or hand tool: Use only on safe, owner-accessible parts; never open electrical or gas equipment.
  • Gloves and eye protection: Choose protection for the specific task; gloves do not prevent electrocution.
  • Manufacturer manual: Check the actual equipment's shutdown, replacement, cleaning, and reassembly procedure.

When a step is unclear, stop and consult the manual or a qualified person. There is no requirement to perform a repair yourself.

PRACTICE AT HOME · STARTER SCHEDULE

Know what to check—and when.

Open a timeframe for ordinary home-care checks. Every Open lesson button opens the exact instructions, even after you have searched for another room. Do only what applies to your home. Follow your specific equipment manual and local conditions. This is a learning schedule, not an automatic reminder or a safety certification.

Renters: tell the property manager about needed repairs and check permission before work. Stop if a task involves danger, inaccessible equipment or unclear instructions.

Every use · small habits
  • Clean the dryer lint screen before or after every load, per the manual.
  • Before using a mower or trimmer, inspect the area and the equipment.
Monthly · safety and leaks
  • Test smoke alarms using the manufacturer’s test function.
  • Inspect the HVAC filter; replace it only as its condition and manual require.
  • Look for visible water leaks without disturbing hazardous areas.
Every few months · appliances
  • Visually examine accessible washer hoses for bulges or leaks.
  • Check accessible dryer venting; arrange qualified cleaning if needed, and do not climb.
  • Check CO alarms at the interval specified by their manufacturer.
Seasonally · weather and comfort
  • After rain, observe drainage from a safe, ground-level location.
  • Check the roof and gutters from the ground after weather; do not climb.
  • Review thermostat schedules when seasons or household routines change.
At least yearly · prepare and prevent
  • Check for a silent toilet leak using the safe dye-test method.
  • Review emergency supplies, contacts and accessibility needs.

Before calling a task complete: check the model-specific instructions, observe the result without risking injury, and record anything unresolved. Stop and seek qualified help for dangerous faults.

Clean kitchen surfacesKitchen · Daily or when soiledRoutine＋

What to have: A surface-appropriate cleaner, water, cloth

Why it matters: Cleaning removes food residue and soil before any optional disinfecting step, helping keep food-contact surfaces usable and hygienic.

Try it safely, step by step

  1. Remove loose food and debris.
  2. Wash with suitable soap/detergent and water; follow the surface manufacturer’s instructions.
  3. Rinse if the product label calls for it and dry with a clean cloth.
  4. Disinfect only when needed, using a labeled product and its required contact time.

Common mistake to avoid: Using disinfectant over grime, combining cleaners, or skipping the product's label instructions.

How to tell it worked: The surface is visibly clean and dry.

Stop and get help if: Never mix household cleaners or disinfectants; stop if a product is damaging the finish.

Clean the refrigerator door sealsKitchen · When visibly dirtyRoutine＋

What to have: Soft cloth, mild dish soap, water

Why it matters: Clean door seals help the refrigerator close properly and keep cold air inside.

Try it safely, step by step

  1. Look for crumbs, grime or gaps around the gasket.
  2. Wipe the rubber seal gently with a damp soapy cloth.
  3. Remove residue and dry it.
  4. Close the door and check that the gasket contacts the cabinet evenly.

Common mistake to avoid: Pulling hard on a seal, using abrasive tools, or assuming every loose gasket can be repaired at home.

How to tell it worked: Door closes fully; seal is clean and flexible.

Stop and get help if: Do not pull hard, cut, or apply heat to a damaged gasket. For torn seals or door alignment, use the appliance manual or service.

Check a toilet for a silent leakBathroom & Plumbing · At least annuallyRoutine＋

What to have: A few drops of food coloring

Why it matters: A silent toilet leak can waste water even when no puddle is visible.

Try it safely, step by step

  1. Remove the tank lid carefully and set it somewhere secure.
  2. Place food coloring in the tank water, not the bowl.
  3. Wait about ten minutes without flushing.
  4. If dye appears in the bowl, suspect a leaking tank seal; flush immediately afterward.

Common mistake to avoid: Adding coloring to the bowl instead of the tank, or forgetting to flush after the test.

How to tell it worked: You know whether the tank is losing water and whether repair is needed.

Stop and get help if: If the tank is cracked, overflowing, or the shutoff does not work, seek qualified help.

What to have: Compatible flapper, towel, model information

Why it matters: A worn flush-valve seal can let tank water continuously slip into the bowl.

Try it safely, step by step

  3. Follow the part instructions to unhook the old flapper and attach the matching replacement; leave appropriate chain slack.

What to have: Surface-approved caulk, painter’s tape, non-damaging removal tool

Why it matters: A sound, cured joint helps prevent splash water from entering a small exposed seam.

Try it safely, step by step

  1. First confirm there is no active leak or significant hidden moisture.
