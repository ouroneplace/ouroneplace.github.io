Before You Agree: Subscription Terms Practice

A small word like trial, renew, or optional can change what you expect to pay. Learn four questions to ask before agreeing to a recurring service, using only fictional offers. No sign-up, payment method, purchase or personal information is involved.

Important: These are invented U.S.-style examples for learning, not actual companies or legally reviewed contracts. The amounts and dates are arbitrary examples, not price quotes, contract interpretation, consumer-rights determinations, or advice to pay or cancel a real service. Laws, cancellation rights, notice requirements and dispute deadlines vary. If terms are missing or unclear, do not give payment details until you understand them. For a real dispute, preserve records and seek the appropriate service or qualified advice.

Four checks before handing over payment details

1 · When would payment begin?

For a free trial, find the exact billing start and what action is needed before the trial ends. Put any real date in a calendar you control, not on this page.

2 · What did you actively choose?

Look for optional extras and preselected boxes. A separate add-on can introduce a second recurring charge.

3 · What is the later price?

Check the renewal amount and frequency, not just the promotional first period. Compare the full terms before deciding.

4 · How would you stop?

Find the cancellation instructions, effective date and confirmation process before enrolling. If you cannot find them, ask the business or walk away.

STOP: Do not treat a countdown timer, sales pressure, an unofficial message or a stranger's cancellation link as verified terms. Never enter your card number, account password or a real contract into this practice page.

Four fictional offers — what should you clarify first?

Each invented offer has one especially important missing or easy-to-miss detail. Read the explanation, then choose its best next question in the private practice below. Model answers work without JavaScript.

A · A seven-day trial

Invented offer: “Try our reading app for $0 today. After seven days, $12 each month unless you cancel.” A sign-up button asks for a payment card, but no calendar date is shown.

WHY: “Free today” may lead to later billing. HOW: Check when the trial begins and ends, what it costs afterward, and what cancellation cutoff applies. CHECK: Find actual start/end dates and keep official terms. COMMON MISTAKE: Assuming a free trial stays free. STOP: Do not enter payment details without understandable terms.

Model question and reason for A

Trial-to-paid billing date. Ask: “Exactly when would billing begin, at what amount, and when must I cancel to avoid a charge?” The fictional seven-day example does not establish a real cancellation deadline.

B · A preselected extra

Invented offer: A $5/month music membership shows an already-checked box for an additional $3/month “premium alerts” feature.

WHY: An optional add-on can create a charge you did not intend. HOW: Read every selected choice and compare the total before continuing. CHECK: Confirm what each checked box authorizes and whether you can decline the extra. COMMON MISTAKE: Clicking “continue” without checking the order. STOP: Do not consent if the choice is unclear.

Model question and reason for B

Optional add-on consent. Ask: “Is this extra service optional, why is it selected, and what is the total recurring cost if I keep or remove it?” Never assume a prechecked option is necessary.

C · A low first-month price

Invented offer: “Streaming for $4 for the first month!” Smaller text says the plan continues at $18/month afterward.

WHY: A promotional price can hide the actual ongoing cost. HOW: Read the full recurring rate and timing before agreeing. CHECK: Distinguish the one-time introductory rate from regular billing. COMMON MISTAKE: Budgeting as if the $4 price repeats. STOP: Do not assume a price-change notice or discount extension without official terms.

Model question and reason for C

Renewal price and frequency. Ask: “What will the service cost after the promotion, how often will it renew, and will I receive clear terms before agreeing?” Do not use the example as a real quote.

D · Cancellation terms are hard to find

Invented offer: A monthly exercise-video service has a clear “Join now” button, but the visible description does not say how to cancel.

WHY: Knowing how to stop a recurring charge matters before you start. HOW: Look for cancellation terms on the official website before agreeing. CHECK: Clarify the method, timing and how confirmation will be documented. COMMON MISTAKE: Assuming deleting an app automatically ends billing. STOP: If the business will not explain the terms, do not sign up merely to discover them.

Model question and reason for D

Cancellation process and confirmation. Ask: “How do I cancel, when does it take effect, and how can I save confirmation?” This example does not decide what any specific law requires.
