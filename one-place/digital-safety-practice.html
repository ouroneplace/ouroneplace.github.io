01 · PRACTICE

An unexpected delivery message

What happened?

You receive a text: “Your package cannot be delivered until you pay a small fee. Open this link now.” No real link is included in this example.

Why it matters

Fake delivery texts often push a quick link and a small payment. A convincing company name does not prove the message is real.

What to do

  1. Do not follow the message's link or attachment.
  2. Check an order using your own saved receipt or the carrier's known app/site.
  3. Report the message through your device or provider, then delete it.

When to stop or get help: If you already provided payment or personal details, contact the relevant bank or provider promptly and use the recovery guide below.

02 · PRACTICE

A voice that sounds familiar asks for money

What happened?

A call appears to come from a relative, says there is an emergency, and asks you to send money immediately. The caller says not to tell anyone.

Why it matters

Caller ID and a familiar-sounding voice can be imitated. Urgency and secrecy are pressure tactics, even if the emergency story sounds emotional.

What to do

  1. Do not send money or disclose account details during the surprise call.
  2. Call the person using a saved number or another independently known channel.
  3. If their safety is genuinely at risk, contact appropriate local emergency services rather than paying a stranger.

When to stop or get help: If anyone is in immediate danger, use the real local emergency service. Do not assume this guide can investigate a caller.

03 · PRACTICE

An account asks you for a sign-in code

What happened?

An unexpected message says your account is locked. It asks you to read back a one-time sign-in code you received on your phone.

Why it matters

One-time codes are meant to confirm access for you, not a stranger. Reused passwords and missing multi-factor authentication can increase account risk.

What to do

  1. Do not share a sign-in code or click an unexpected account link.
  2. Open your known app or saved site; review recent logins and security alerts.
  3. Use a unique strong password and enable multi-factor authentication where available.

When to stop or get help: If an account is compromised, use its official recovery process. If you cannot access it, contact official support through a verified route.

04 · PRACTICE

You already sent money

What happened?

After sending money for an offer, you suspect the seller was pretending to be legitimate. Another person promises recovery if you pay an upfront fee.

Why it matters

Recovery promises can be another scam. Acting quickly with the real bank, card issuer or payment service may improve the chance of intervention, but refunds are not guaranteed.

What to do

  1. Use your card, bank or payment app's official contact route immediately.
  2. Explain the suspected scam, ask what reversal/dispute options exist, and preserve relevant receipts.
  3. Report fraud to your country's official agency. In the U.S., use ReportFraud.ftc.gov; identity-theft recovery starts at IdentityTheft.gov.

When to stop or get help: No website can promise a refund or establish legal rights for every country. Contact relevant professional and official services for your situation.

What is the best immediate next step?

Use this in real life: pause, verify, protect, respond

  1. Pause: Notice surprise contact, secrecy, unusual urgency or requests for money or codes.
  2. Verify: Reach the person or organization through an address, app or number you already trust—not one supplied by the message.
  3. Protect: Keep codes private, use unique strong passwords, multi-factor authentication and software updates.
  4. Respond: If you shared money or data, promptly contact the real service and report what happened using your country's official options.

Do not assume every urgent message is fraudulent or every professional-looking message is real. The important skill is independent verification.
