Alerts to Safe Action Practice

Learn three different skills: build a way to receive official alerts, understand a tornado watch versus a warning, and avoid flooded evacuation routes. The examples are fictional and the answer checks are optional. Read the steps or practice a choice; nothing is saved.

In a real emergency, leave this lesson. This page is NOT a live warning feed, a safe-route finder, a shelter locator, or an emergency-response service. Immediately follow the current instructions of local emergency officials and first responders. Call your local emergency number if life is in immediate danger (911 in the U.S.). Hazard instructions differ: a tornado warning may require immediate shelter; a flood evacuation order may require leaving by a verified safe route. Never use a generic quiz to overrule an official instruction.

U.S.-focused learning, not location-specific directions. People in mobile homes, vehicles, remote areas, or with disability, mobility, language, hearing or medical-equipment needs should confirm their actual shelter, warning access and assistance plan with local services ahead of time. There is no guarantee any single alert, route or contact will work.

Find more than one way to hear a warning

Why this matters

A phone can be off, out of power, out of coverage, or unable to receive a particular alert. Planning a second route before trouble helps people hear and understand instructions.

Fictional situation

A fictional household receives emergency updates only through one person's phone. That person may be away overnight.

How to prepare or respond

  1. Check official local emergency management alerts and whether your phone can receive Wireless Emergency Alerts.
  2. Choose a second accessible way to hear announcements, such as NOAA Weather Radio or local radio/TV, and verify it actually works where you live.
  3. Agree on a respectful check-in route for anyone who needs accessible formats, assistance, language support, or a backup way to communicate.

Common mistake

Do not assume every watch or warning triggers a phone message, that outdoor sirens are heard indoors, or that a contact will always be reachable.

When to stop or seek help: If an active warning requires immediate action, follow the issuing authority now. Do not spend critical time setting up a new device or completing this lesson.

Match the message to the action

Why this matters

For tornadoes, a watch means conditions favor tornadoes; a warning means a tornado has been detected or reported and protective action is urgent. Do not generalize these labels to every hazard.

Fictional situation

A fictional person inside a sturdy home has already identified a safe room. A Tornado Warning is issued for their location.

How to prepare or respond

  1. Before severe weather, identify the safest available shelter for that specific building and household; consider mobility/access needs early.
  2. During a tornado watch, review the plan, monitor official updates, and get ready to act.
  3. During a tornado warning for your location, take shelter promptly in a basement, safe room or small interior room on the lowest floor of a sturdy building, away from windows; follow current official instructions.

Common mistake

Do not treat a tornado warning as a chance to go outdoors to watch, drive around to inspect damage, or finish an online lesson.

When to stop or seek help: Different structures need different plans. Mobile homes, vehicles and outdoor situations need a nearby substantial shelter; people with mobility or access needs should arrange safe routes in advance. This page cannot determine a safe route or building in real time.

LESSON 3 OF 3 · LEARN / PRACTICE / STOP

Follow officials, never force a flooded crossing

Why this matters

Floodwater can conceal washed-out roads, current, debris or contamination. An evacuation order may require leaving the hazard zone, but it never makes flooded roads safe to cross.

Fictional situation

Local officials tell a fictional household to leave a flood-prone area. While heading toward the official evacuation route, the driver discovers water covering a road.

How to prepare or respond

  1. Before flood season, learn where official alerts and evacuation routes are published, and identify accessible transportation and a contact route if your household needs help.
  2. When local authorities order evacuation, follow their current instructions early enough if safe; routes and shelters may change.
  3. If you encounter a flooded road or barrier, do not enter it. Turn back if safe, seek a verified alternative from local authorities, and contact emergency services when in immediate danger.

Common mistake

Do not estimate safe depth by looking at the road or follow another vehicle through floodwater.

When to stop or seek help: For fast-rising water or immediate danger, seek live local emergency help now. Avoid entering floodwaters on foot or by vehicle; do not rely on this page to choose a destination or route. Other hazards, including tornadoes, may call for immediate shelter rather than travel.

Remember: receive, understand, act

  1. Receive: confirm official alerts through more than one reachable channel.
  2. Understand: read what hazard, place and timing the alert actually names.
  3. Act: take the hazard-specific protective step from officials. Stop the exercise when an actual event is happening.
