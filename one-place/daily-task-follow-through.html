One Task, Four Moves

Getting organized is more than making a list. Learn to prepare one ordinary task, actually carry it out, verify what changed and choose a reminder for next time.

Four short practice decisions · No grade · No account · No personal details required · Optional editable example

Respect your capacity and safety. You can adapt, pause, ask for help or stop. Never risk a fall, block a doorway, overexert yourself or reveal private details just to complete a routine. This is general learning, not a clinical assessment or a demand to be productive.

One everyday situation

Tomorrow, Sam is attending a free community class. The class note says to bring a notebook and pencil. Sam has a reusable bag and limited time and energy today. The home is shared, so a bag cannot block the door.

Can Sam prepare without buying anything, guessing what is needed or exhausting themselves? Practice the four moves. There is no single routine that fits everyone.

Outcome: Sam should be able to find the actual requirement, put the items in an accessible location, verify they are ready, and choose a respectful reminder.

Move 1 of 4 · Prepare

Know what to prepare and why

Why: Planning from the original instruction prevents wasted effort. Choose a place and an action that match your reach, ability, time and resources.

How: Read or listen to the class instructions, identify the two needed objects, and choose one small starting action. Ask someone you trust to read or translate instructions if needed.

What is the most reliable first step for Sam?

Read why checking instructions matters

Sam has an original class note. The concrete requirement is a notebook and pencil, not unspecified new products. A safe and reachable place makes the next step easier; asking for help with reading is a legitimate adaptation.

Move 2 of 4 · Do

Perform a real, manageable step

Why: A plan is only a starting point. An accessible, voluntary first action helps move the task toward completion without forcing a difficult pace.

How: Locate the notebook and pencil, place them together in or beside the bag if reachable, and take a break as needed. If an item is inaccessible, change the method or ask for help rather than taking a physical risk.

Sam cannot comfortably reach a high shelf. What next?

Read an accessible action example

Sam can use an available accessible item or ask for assistance with consent. A risky climb is not necessary; fatigue and mobility needs are valid reasons to pause or change the task.

Move 3 of 4 · Check

Verify the real result

Why: Feeling busy and having completed the requirement are not the same. A quick check can catch a missing item while there is still time to adjust.

How: Compare the actual notebook and pencil with the class note. Confirm that both are in a reachable, permitted place and that the route stays clear. If one is missing, note a practical alternative rather than claiming the task is finished.

Which check shows the preparation is complete?

Read a verification example

The observable result is notebook and pencil ready, in a location Sam can use, with no blocked path. An item count and a safe location matter more than how busy Sam felt.

Move 4 of 4 · Remember

Leave a useful cue, not a demand

Why: An external cue can reduce what you need to remember. It should be easy for you to notice without exposing private information to other people.

How: Choose a written word, picture, tactile marker, voice reminder or existing calendar cue that fits your access needs and daily routine. A helper can assist with consent; no smartphone or paid app is required.

Which reminder is the most useful to explore?

Read a privacy-aware reminder example

A short cue like “class bag” in a place Sam can perceive may be enough. It need not include a location, identity, private appointment information or a paid device. If a cue stops working, change it.
