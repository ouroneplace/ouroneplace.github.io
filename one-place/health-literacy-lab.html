Read an OTC medicine label

Why it matters

A label separates what is in a product, its intended uses, safety warnings and directions. Reading only the front of a package can miss important safety information.

What to know

On U.S. over-the-counter medicines, Drug Facts commonly lists Active ingredient, Uses, Warnings and Directions. Prescription instructions look different. This exercise never supplies a dose.

How to practice

  1. Locate the active ingredient on each package, even when brand names differ.
  2. Read warnings and directions together rather than guessing from the product name.
  3. Ask a pharmacist about unclear instructions or possible overlap with other medicines.

Check the result

You can point to the active ingredient, warnings and directions, and identify one question for a pharmacist.

Common mistake: Assuming differently branded packages must contain different active ingredients.

When to stop and ask: If a label is unclear, or you have concerns about interactions or health conditions, seek individual guidance instead of improvising.

Ask safer medicine questions

Why it matters

Instructions and missed-dose guidance can differ greatly between medicines. A general quiz cannot tell someone what to take.

What to know

List your medicines, supplements and allergies for your care team. Ask the purpose, instructions, interactions, possible side effects and whom to contact.

How to practice

  1. Write down what is unclear instead of guessing.
  2. Check your prescription or package instructions and ask a pharmacist or prescriber for product-specific guidance.
  3. Repeat the answer in your own words and confirm what to do if a problem occurs.

Check the result

You can name the question you will ask and who can give personalized guidance.

Common mistake: Applying a missed-dose rule learned for one medicine to every other medicine.

When to stop and ask: Do not double, stop, restart or adjust medicine based on this lesson. Seek qualified guidance for individual instructions.

Check online health claims

Why it matters

Popularity, testimonials and a confident headline do not prove a health claim is reliable or appropriate for everyone.

What to know

Look for who runs a site, who pays for it, when the content was reviewed, and credible supporting evidence. Compare with another independent source.

How to practice

  1. Identify the writer, sponsoring organization and purpose.
  2. Look for dates, research and limitations—not just advertisements or testimonials.
  3. Compare an independent trustworthy source and ask a clinician before changing care.

Check the result

You can say who benefits from the claim, how current the evidence is, and where else you checked.

Common mistake: Treating social shares, testimonials or dramatic promises as clinical proof.

When to stop and ask: Do not replace established care or buy a product based solely on an unverified claim.

LESSON 4 OF 4 · LEARN / PRACTICE / CHECK

Plan for screening results

Why it matters

Screening can identify potential problems before symptoms, but individual testing choices depend on health history and risk factors.

What to know

Ask what a test is for, its possible benefits and harms, alternatives, what a result may mean, and who explains the result.

How to practice

  1. Ask why a screening is being considered for your circumstances.
  2. Discuss benefits, limitations and preferences with your care team.
  3. Agree when results should arrive, how they will be explained and whom to contact if they do not arrive.

Check the result

You can name the result contact person, the expected time and the next step if no result arrives.

Common mistake: Assuming that no call means a normal result or that everyone needs the same screening schedule.

When to stop and ask: Do not use this practice to diagnose a condition or determine your personal screening eligibility.
