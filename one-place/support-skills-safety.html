Support Skills & Safety Lab

Learn how to offer support, respect boundaries, recognize when ordinary conversation is not enough, and care for your own limits. Four fictional situations, one choice at a time. No account or personal story required.

Urgent help comes first: If someone may hurt themselves or someone else, or you cannot keep them safe, skip these lessons and contact appropriate local crisis or emergency services now. In the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support or call 911 for an immediate emergency. Outside the U.S., use your country's verified local crisis/emergency service. This page cannot assess danger, send alerts, or contact responders.

These examples are for human understanding, not diagnosis, therapy, or a substitute for qualified care. They are not a test of whether someone is a good person. Never enter real private health details here.

01 · PRACTICE A DECISION

Someone you care about seems withdrawn

A fictional situation

A friend has canceled plans and seems unusually quiet. They have not said they are in danger.

Why this matters

A change in behavior can have many explanations. You cannot diagnose someone by observing one sign, but a respectful check-in can make room for support.

A practical response

  1. Choose a private, comfortable moment and speak without judgment.
  2. Say what you noticed, not what you assume: “I've noticed you've been quieter lately.”
  3. Ask “Would you like to talk, or would something else help?” and listen.

When to stop or seek help: If you hear threats of self-harm, suicide or immediate danger, move to urgent help rather than this ordinary check-in.

02 · PRACTICE A DECISION

Someone asks for space

A fictional situation

A family member says, “I appreciate you asking, but I don't want to explain everything right now.”

Why this matters

Support respects autonomy. Pressuring someone to talk can damage trust, and a supportive relationship does not require constant conversation.

A practical response

  1. Acknowledge their preference and do not make it about your own need for reassurance.
  2. Offer practical help or an agreed later check-in, without insisting they accept.
  3. If you are worried about safety, ask directly and seek appropriate help rather than relying on assumptions.

When to stop or seek help: A credible immediate safety risk changes the response: safety and qualified crisis support take priority over ordinary privacy expectations.

03 · PRACTICE A DECISION

Someone says they may hurt themselves tonight

A fictional situation

A person you know tells you, “I have a plan to hurt myself tonight. Please don't tell anyone.”

Why this matters

An expressed near-term self-harm plan is a serious warning sign. A practice worksheet cannot assess risk or replace urgent human help.

A practical response

  1. Take the statement seriously and respond calmly, without arguing or blaming.
  2. If there is immediate danger, contact the local emergency number. In the U.S., call 911 for an emergency; 988 provides crisis support.
  3. If safe, stay connected and follow professional responders' guidance. Never promise to keep a dangerous plan secret.

When to stop or seek help: Skip all website exercises in an active crisis. This site cannot contact emergency services, assess risk or provide a live response.

04 · PRACTICE A DECISION

You are supporting someone and feel overwhelmed

A fictional situation

You have been helping a loved one for days. You are tired and notice you are becoming impatient. There is no known immediate danger right now.

Why this matters

Reliable support also includes sustainable boundaries. Caring about someone does not mean one person must meet every need alone.

A practical response

  1. Name your own capacity: “I can talk for a short while, but I need rest afterward.”
  2. Ask what kind of practical help is most useful and which others can safely share responsibilities.
  3. Make space for your own rest or professional support if needed.

When to stop or seek help: If the person is in immediate danger or a dependent person may be left unsafe, contact qualified or emergency support before stepping away.

Remember: notice, ask, listen, connect

  1. Notice a change without labeling or diagnosing.
  2. Ask permission and what kind of help the person actually wants.
  3. Listen without forcing a conversation; respect boundaries except where there is immediate safety risk.
  4. Connect with trusted practical, professional or crisis support when needed. Seek your own support too.

Caring can look different across cultures and families. Ask what is welcome rather than assuming a single response fits everyone.
