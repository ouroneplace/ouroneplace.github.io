Stories Are Gifts, Not Obligations

A life story belongs to the person who lived it. Practice asking permission, listening without pushing and agreeing on what happens to someone's words before recording, writing or sharing them.

Three fictional situations · No grade · No account · Optional editable invitation · No recording or publication

Respect the right not to share. Someone can say no, skip a question, change their mind or stop. Never pressure a person to recount loss, trauma or other private events. If recording or publishing real material, separate permission and release requirements may apply under local law or the archive's policies. This page is education, not a legal release or advice about a person's capacity to consent.
1. Invite clearlySay why you are asking, whether you want notes or recording, and who might see the result.
2. Listen respectfullyLet the storyteller control the topic, language, time and whether to continue.
3. Confirm useAsk before quoting, recording, keeping, distributing or publishing a story. Agree on review and limits.

A fictional family story

Alex hopes to learn from older relative Morgan about making music with neighbors years ago. Alex thinks the family would enjoy preserving the memory. Morgan is not automatically interested in speaking, being recorded, or having the story circulated. Alex can invite without obligation and without promising privacy beyond their control.

Use these scenarios as rehearsal. Actual family relationships, cultural knowledge, disability access, power differences, age and legal rules vary. The safest real step may be to stop.

Move 1 · Invite

Ask for a conversation—not ownership of a story

Why: A person needs to know what is being requested and how their words may be used before freely deciding. A warm family connection is not itself consent to be interviewed or recorded.

How: Explain the purpose, the proposed audience, whether you would take notes or record, and that saying no is fine. Ask what the other person wants. Do not start a recording while asking.

What is a respectful invitation for Morgan?

Read a clear-invitation example

Alex can explain the goal and ask whether Morgan wants a conversation at all. A note, audio recording, transcript, family-only story and public post are different uses. Permission to chat does not automatically authorize recording, editing or sharing. A refusal is a complete answer.

Move 2 · Listen

Let the storyteller change the plan

Why: Consent is an ongoing conversation, not one question at the beginning. A story belongs to more than one moment; the person may become tired or no longer want to discuss a sensitive subject.

How: Use open, non-leading questions such as “What do you remember enjoying?” Allow silence. If Morgan says a topic is uncomfortable, accept it; a pause, another topic or stopping are all valid.

Morgan answers a few questions, then says, “I don't want to talk about the disagreement that ended the music group.”

Which response respects Morgan's boundary?

Read a boundary-respecting example

Morgan can decline a subject, take a break or end the conversation. Alex can listen without filling in details, blaming Morgan, or treating another person's account as Morgan's. If Morgan no longer wants the session, Alex stops.

Move 3 · Confirm

Agree on what may be kept or shared

Why: Agreeing to tell a story is not the same as agreeing to publication. Recordings and written memories can reveal facts about other people and may spread beyond their intended audience.

How: Before sharing, describe exactly what is in the draft, who will have access and whether it can be copied. Offer the storyteller a chance to review, clarify, remove sensitive parts, restrict access or decide against release. Do not guarantee you can retract material from other people's devices after it is shared.

Morgan agreed to private family notes. Later, Alex wants to post a quote with Morgan's name on a public social page. Morgan has not been asked about that use.

What should Alex do before publishing the quote?

Read a permission-before-sharing example

A family-only note is not permission for a public post. Even an unnamed quote might identify someone by its details. Alex should agree on the words, attribution, limits and audience before any new use. If Morgan says no, Alex does not publish. This practice page does not create a legal agreement or transfer rights.

Optional transfer to real conversations

Put a respectful invitation in your own words

Try a generic invitation. Name the purpose, proposed method and audience; explain that refusal and stopping are allowed. Do not enter real family histories, contact details, identifying names or sensitive information here.

Read a complete example invitation with or without JavaScript

“I would love to hear what making music with neighbors was like for you. Would you be comfortable talking about it sometime? I was thinking of making written notes for a small family keepsake, not recording or posting online. Please say no or skip anything you don't want to discuss. Before any notes are shared, I would ask you to review them and agree on who can read them. If you change your mind, we can stop.”
