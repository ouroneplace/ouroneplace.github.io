Small Business Records Practice

Before choosing bookkeeping software, learn how to recognize a business transaction, find its supporting document, and tell business activity from personal spending. Practice with four invented records—no account, bank connection, or real business information.

Important boundaries: All four examples are fictional and use U.S. dollar amounts only to make the exercise easy to read. This is an introductory sorting exercise, not a complete ledger, tax calculation, statement of profit, accounting advice, or a claim that a particular expense is deductible. Cash received, invoices, refunds, inventory, mixed-use purchases, tax and payroll all may require different treatment. For real records, use applicable rules and qualified help when necessary. No data is collected or stored here.

What a usable record needs

1 · Identify the event

Ask what actually happened: customer payment received, item purchased for work, personal use, or money returned to a customer. Keep the story factual.

2 · Keep evidence

Find the receipt, invoice, bill, refund notice, deposit slip or relevant statement. A payment record alone may not show why a cost happened.

3 · Write a clear description

Record the date, amount, purpose, source and the related document or reference. Use a consistent system that suits the business and its legal obligations.

4 · Check and separate

Review possible duplicates, refunds and personal spending. Keep private customer or employee details out of shared practice material. Ask a qualified adviser about uncertain categorization.

When to stop: Never invent missing invoices, turn personal purchases into business deductions, or treat this page as a filing system. If an actual record is disputed or legally significant, preserve the original information and use the right professional or agency.

Four fictional transactions

Every case includes the underlying event, a suggested document to locate, and an expandable model answer. Try to classify them yourself first, then use the private practice form below. The model answers are readable even with JavaScript disabled.

A · Customer payment received

Invented event: On October 2, a customer pays $80 for a service already completed. A receipt was issued. No name or account number is shown.

WHY: The business needs a factual record of money received and its source. HOW: Link the event to a receipt or payment confirmation, with date and amount.

CHECK: Verify the recorded amount and source match the actual supporting document. COMMON MISTAKE: Counting the same payment twice. STOP: Do not infer the amount of taxable income from this example alone.

Model answer and supporting record for A

Customer payment. Keep the service receipt or payment record. A real business should follow its accounting method and applicable tax rules; the invented $80 is not a tax conclusion.

B · Supplies bought for a customer job

Invented event: On October 3, the business pays $24 for supplies used in a customer job. The store receipt describes the items.

WHY: The purpose of a payment matters as much as the amount. HOW: Keep an itemized receipt and a clear explanation of its business purpose.

CHECK: Look for mixed personal use, inventory or asset rules before making tax decisions. COMMON MISTAKE: Assuming all purchases are immediately deductible. STOP: Ask a qualified tax or accounting adviser when treatment is uncertain.

Model answer and supporting record for B

Business purchase. Keep the receipt and business purpose. This practice label does not establish eligibility for a current tax deduction.

C · Personal groceries paid using a business card

Invented event: On October 3, a sole proprietor accidentally pays $35 for family groceries using a business payment card. The receipt is for personal food.

WHY: Paying from a business account does not turn personal shopping into a business expense. HOW: Flag the transaction accurately for correction or appropriate owner-use classification.

CHECK: Keep the statement and receipt to explain what happened. COMMON MISTAKE: Calling every charge on a business card a deductible cost. STOP: Treatment varies by entity and tax circumstances; do not invent an accounting adjustment here.

Model answer and supporting record for C

Personal spending — keep separate. Preserve the receipt and accurately flag the use of the business card. Do not classify family groceries as a business cost merely because of the payment account.

D · Money returned to a customer

Invented event: On October 4, the business returns $20 to a customer after a documented adjustment to a prior sale. The refund receipt references the original transaction.

WHY: Returning money is not a second new sale. HOW: Link the refund record to the original transaction and preserve the reason and amount.

CHECK: Confirm it was not recorded twice and that the customer received the correct adjustment. COMMON MISTAKE: Ignoring a refund when reviewing the full history. STOP: Refund, sales-tax and bookkeeping treatments can differ; consult applicable requirements.

Model answer and supporting record for D

Customer refund or adjustment. Keep the original receipt and the refund confirmation together. This exercise does not compute taxes or determine financial-statement treatment.
