WHAT THIS IS
A short set of original introductory lessons about how to think, live,
learn and care for people and the planet. It is NOT all of human knowledge.
It is not emergency response, medical treatment, legal advice or a substitute
for local expertise. This file can be saved and printed. It does not require
an internet connection merely to read the text; links to sources will require
access to those sources. This guide will not update itself after download.

THE BASIC METHOD
Know what the question is.
Understand why it matters.
Check the evidence and important limits.
Try one safe, manageable practice.
Apply what is appropriate to your situation.
Revisit when facts or circumstances change.

1. SAFETY AND URGENT HELP
-------------------------
An online explanation cannot see your home, injuries, environment or risks.
Start with what you can directly observe.

1) Is someone in immediate danger? Contact appropriate local emergency
   assistance if so. Do not finish a website exercise in place of urgent help.
2) Separate direct observation from rumors, interpretations and guesses.
3) Avoid actions that could increase danger. Use trusted local specialists
   when a hazard exceeds your knowledge or ability.

TRY IT: Identify the real emergency-help contact that applies where you live.
Local numbers and services differ worldwide.

LIMIT: This is general safety reasoning, not first-aid training or a promise
that emergency services are available everywhere.

2. UNDERSTANDING HEALTH INFORMATION
-----------------------------------
Health literacy includes finding, understanding, evaluating and using
information. It is not simply memorizing medical words.

1) Name the actual question or decision and whether it is urgent.
2) Check who produced the health information, when it was reviewed,
   what evidence supports it and whom it applies to.
3) Ask a qualified professional what benefits, risks and alternatives
   apply to your circumstances before changing treatments.

TRY IT: Practice saying, "What do we know, what is uncertain, and what
should I ask my care team next?"

LIMIT: Never stop prescribed medication or care because an online message
seems convincing. This guide provides no personalized treatment.

Original background: WHO health literacy
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/health-literacy

3. CHECKING A CLAIM
-------------------
A claim can sound convincing without being well supported. A dramatic image,
a repeated rumor or a confident AI response is not proof.

1) State the exact claim. Is it a measured finding, interpretation,
   prediction, value judgment or personal story?
2) Find the original evidence. Check the author, date, actual observation,
   method, context and weaknesses.
3) Seek competent independent corroboration. If important information
   is missing, say "I don't know yet" rather than pretending certainty.

TRY IT: Ask what other explanation fits the same observation, and what
additional evidence would help distinguish them.

LIMIT: Science is vital for empirical questions. Ethical, cultural and
personal decisions also require listening and transparent values.

Original background: UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science
https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-science

4. LIVING WITH OTHER PEOPLE
--------------------------
Respect does not mean automatic agreement, silent submission or ignoring
your own needs. People bring different cultures, life stories and abilities.

1) Describe what happened before concluding another person's motive.
2) Ask what kind of conversation or help is welcome.
3) Express your own need clearly. Consider a practical option that respects
   both people's safety, autonomy and boundaries.

TRY IT: "I noticed _____. How do you understand it? I need _____. What
might work for us, if we both want to discuss it?"

LIMIT: Some situations are unsafe. Do not pressure a person to reconcile
with someone harming them. Qualified help may be necessary.

Background on access to learning and cultural dignity:
https://www.ifla.org/public-library-manifesto/

5. EARTH AND LIVING SYSTEMS
---------------------------
Air, water, soil, climate and other living things support human and
nonhuman life. A local action can affect nearby communities and ecosystems.

1) Name one nearby life-supporting system (water source, soil, habitat).
2) Ask where it comes from, what organisms depend on it, and what threatens it.
3) Choose one locally appropriate way to avoid unnecessary harm.
   Verify first before changing waterways, handling wildlife or releasing
   plants or animals into the environment.

TRY IT: Notice a living thing in your surroundings and ask what it needs
to thrive without human interference.

LIMIT: A simple observation is not a complete environmental assessment.
Local habitats, regulations and ecological tradeoffs differ.

Original background: UNEP Nature-based Solutions
https://www.unep.org/en/what-we-do/nature-and-land-action/nature-based-solutions/overview-nature-based-solutions

6. OUR SOLAR SYSTEM AND THE UNIVERSE
------------------------------------
The Sun is a star. Earth is the third planet from the Sun. Our solar system
includes eight recognized planets, moons and many smaller objects.

1) Distinguish observation from explanation: seeing a moving point of
   light does not reveal its cause.
2) Find out what was measured, with which instruments, and how uncertainty
   was considered.
3) Compare explanations using credible astronomy evidence.

TRY IT: Explain the difference between a star and a planet in your own words.
List one question about space that you would like to investigate.

LIMIT: The universe is vast and science is still discovering new phenomena.
An extraordinary theory needs evidence, not merely an unusual photograph.

Original background: NASA Solar System Facts
https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/solar-system-facts/

THREE REASONING PRACTICES
-------------------------

CASE A: A post claims one drink cures every illness and urges people to
stop medical care. Should you believe it because someone speaks confidently?
SAFER REASONING: No. Look for high-quality evidence, consider harms and ask
qualified clinicians before changing personal care.

CASE B: A blurry sky image is described as proof of extraterrestrial visitors.
Does the photograph alone establish the cause?
SAFER REASONING: No. It shows something that may deserve investigation.
Date, context, original measurements and independent explanations matter.

CASE C: A forwarded message claims a nearby water supply is unsafe, but it
has no identifiable dated notice. Should you ignore it or assume it applies
everywhere?
SAFER REASONING: Neither. Check your known local utility or public-health
authority using a trustworthy independent route. If a credible warning is
current and relevant, follow local safety instructions.

HOW TO KEEP LEARNING
--------------------
Start with one need or one genuine question. Read a short explanation. Try
a safe practice. Check what you understood. Ask where the information came
from. Revise when evidence changes. Share responsibly.

The next generation deserves not only answers, but the ability to question
and improve those answers. Humans, communities and nature are partners in
this work; a website must remain a guide rather than an absolute authority.
