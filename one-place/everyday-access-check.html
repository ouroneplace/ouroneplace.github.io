Everyday Access Check

Find one barrier in a daily routine. Choose an accommodation that respects the person's preferences. Make a small, safe plan you can check.

For older adults, disabled people, caregivers, and anyone with a temporary or changing limitation. A barrier is often in the environment or communication—not a failure of the person.

Safety first: If someone is in immediate danger, use the local emergency response system (911 in the U.S.; numbers vary elsewhere). Do not attempt an unsafe lift or transfer, change medication, make structural alterations, or ignore sudden new symptoms because of this guide. Seek qualified local help for these situations.
Respect choice: Ask whether help is wanted and which approach works. Do not move a wheelchair, walker, cane or communication device without permission. A family relationship does not automatically grant medical or legal decision-making authority.

2. Learn to spot barriers and respond

Use the section that matches your concern. These are starting checks, not a test of independent mobility, clinical assessment, or building-code compliance.

Getting around and moving safely

Why
Blocked paths, poor lighting, and unsuitable surfaces can increase falls or prevent a mobility device from passing.
Notice
Look from a safe position for loose objects, cords, wet floors, narrow turns and stairs without reliable support.
Try
Ask the person about their usual route. Keep paths clear, plan rest stops, and ask a qualified professional about mobility aids, transfers, ramps or grab bars when needed.
Check
Can the person use the route and reach essentials with their preferred support, without pressure to take a risk? Do not perform a risky trial to find out.
Common mistake
Moving a mobility device without consent, or assuming a temporary grab bar or improvised ramp is safe.
Stop and seek help
If the route requires lifting, an unsafe transfer, structural changes, or the person has fallen, is newly unsteady, or has concerning symptoms, seek appropriate professional help.

Seeing routes and information

Why
Dim light, glare, low contrast and hard-to-read labels can hide obstacles and important information.
Notice
Check light at entrances and steps, readable labels, and whether directions rely only on color or tiny print.
Try
Ask whether brighter indirect light, larger print, higher contrast, an audio option, or an agreed guiding method would help.
Check
Can the person find the correct place or instruction using their preferred format without guessing?
Common mistake
Grabbing someone's arm or moving belongings instead of asking first.
Stop and seek help
Sudden vision loss or other acute symptoms need urgent medical assessment. Persistent vision concerns warrant appropriate professional advice.

Hearing communication and alerts

Why
Spoken-only instructions or alarms can leave people without critical information.
Notice
Look for loud background noise, no captions or transcript, and emergency alerts available only as sound.
Try
Ask the person's preferred communication: writing, captions, an interpreter, visual or vibration alerts, or a quieter space. Confirm that important messages were received.
Check
Can the person explain or demonstrate the agreed next step using their own preferred method?
Common mistake
Assuming everyone uses sign language or that louder speech alone solves the barrier.
Stop and seek help
For safety-critical alarms or accessibility services, consult the responsible provider or qualified installer; do not rely on an untested device.

Understanding information and using technology

Why
Long instructions, fast speech and inaccessible forms can block otherwise simple tasks.
Notice
Look for confusing steps, passwords requested by helpers, time pressure, small controls or no non-digital option.
Try
Offer one step at a time, plain words, larger text or read-aloud support if requested. Ask providers for accessible formats or a phone/in-person alternative.
Check
Can the person state the next action in their own words or preferred format, without being rushed?
Common mistake
Taking over a phone or account, sharing passwords, or assuming difficulty means the person lacks decision-making ability.
Stop and seek help
If the task involves consent, benefits, finances, sensitive records or legal authority, confirm rules with the responsible service or qualified adviser.

Limited energy or a temporary limitation

Why
Fatigue, injury, recovery and changing capacity can make a familiar routine difficult for a time.
Notice
Look for long standing periods, distant seating, no pause options, heavy items or a route with no accessible alternative.
Try
Ask about pacing, rest breaks, seated tasks, closer placement of essentials or rescheduling. Confirm accommodations with the place or service.
Check
Can the activity be completed without pressure, avoidable strain or loss of autonomy?
Common mistake
Pushing through dizziness, pain, weakness or exhaustion to finish a planned task.
Stop and seek help
New or worsening symptoms, unsafe movement or unclear recovery restrictions call for professional advice; urgent symptoms require urgent care.

3. Practice one safe decision

Fictional situation: A person using a walker wants to reach the kitchen, but boxes partly block the hall.

What is the best first action?

Choose one response, then check the reasoning.

Show the explanation without using the activity

First ask the person and arrange a clear, safe route. Never pressure someone to squeeze past a hazard or remove their mobility device. A new fall risk or unsafe transfer calls for appropriate help. This is reasoning practice, not physical safety certification.

4. Paper or offline worksheet

No device or account required. Write these answers on paper or print this page. Do not record diagnoses or identification numbers here.

  1. Which daily task is blocked?
  2. What is the barrier in the environment or communication?
  3. What does the person prefer? Have I asked?
  4. What is one safe change or accommodation to confirm?
  5. How will we check that it works without taking a risk?
  6. Who should we contact if the barrier cannot be safely resolved?

A useful outcome: One identified barrier, one consent-based next step, and a clear stop/help boundary.
