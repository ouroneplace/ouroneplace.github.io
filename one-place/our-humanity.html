THE INNER LIFE

◉ Understanding ourselves

No two people experience life identically. Our thoughts, memories, feelings, bodies, identities, values and choices keep changing.

↑ Top
Our amazing capacity to learn＋

We adapt, observe, experiment and pass knowledge between generations.

Our vulnerability: Confidence can outrun understanding; a familiar explanation may feel true before it has been checked.

Try this: Try: Explain one interesting idea in your own words, then look for one thing you might have missed.

Background reading: National Academies · How People Learn II ↗

Emotions are signals, not orders＋

Fear can warn us, sadness can reveal loss, anger can reveal perceived unfairness, and joy can draw us toward connection.

Our vulnerability: Feelings are real experiences but do not always tell us the full story of what happened or what another person intended.

Try this: Try: Name what you feel, what you know, and what you still need to learn before acting.

Background reading: National Academies · How People Learn II ↗

Attention and the busy mind＋

Our minds notice threats, rewards, novelty and patterns; focused attention helps us learn and create.

Our vulnerability: Stress and distraction can narrow what we notice. The loudest thought is not necessarily the wisest.

Try this: Try: Give a small task your full attention for a few minutes; notice how the experience changes.

Background reading: National Academies · How People Learn II ↗

Memory, stories and mistakes＋

Memory helps us navigate life and gives our experiences meaning.

Our vulnerability: Memories are reconstructed; confidence is not the same as accuracy. Two people can remember one event differently.

Try this: Try: Separate 'what I witnessed' from 'what I inferred' when retelling a disagreement.

Background reading: National Academies · How People Learn II ↗

Self-respect and accountability＋

We can acknowledge strengths, set boundaries, accept consequences and change behavior.

Our vulnerability: Self-criticism can become cruelty toward ourselves; pride can prevent honest correction.

Try this: Try: Say, 'This action was wrong; what will I do to repair it?' without turning the mistake into your entire identity.

Background reading: National Academies · Motivation to learn ↗

Belonging without losing ourselves＋

People often flourish when they feel valued, capable and free to choose.

Our vulnerability: Seeking approval at any price can erase our preferences, while isolation can make life harder.

Try this: Try: Identify one preference you can express respectfully and one connection you would like to nurture.

Background reading: WHO · Social connection ↗

Meaning, faith and uncertainty＋

People find purpose through relationships, service, creativity, faith, inquiry, work and many other paths.

Our vulnerability: A strong personal belief can help us live well; it can also become pressure if we demand that others share it.

Try this: Try: Describe a value you want to live by without requiring anyone else to agree with its source.

Background reading: OECD · How’s Life? 2024 ↗

LOVE AND CONNECTION

♡ Living with one another

Affection is powerful; trust, consent, fairness, boundaries and repair make it safer. Nobody must stay in harm to prove love.

↑ Top
Love beyond the feeling＋

Love can include attention, tenderness, loyalty, mutual support and deliberate action.

Our vulnerability: Love alone doesn't prove compatibility, erase harm, or justify control and coercion.

Try this: Try: Ask whether your actions respect the other person's autonomy as well as your own.

Background reading: CDC · Preventing partner violence ↗

Listening and being heard＋

We can notice another person's perspective without surrendering our own.

Our vulnerability: When defensive, we may listen only for a point to refute. Understanding isn't agreement.

Try this: Try: Reflect the other person's concern accurately before presenting your response.

Background reading: WHO · Social connection ↗

Boundaries and consent＋

Good boundaries create room for two people to have needs, preferences and limits.

Our vulnerability: Without boundaries, care can turn into resentment; without respect, limits can turn into demands on others.

Try this: Try: State what you will do, not what another person must feel: 'I can discuss this when we both speak calmly.'

Background reading: CDC · Preventing partner violence ↗

Conflict, anger and repair＋

Arguments can reveal unmet needs or important differences; repair can restore trust after harm.

Our vulnerability: Insults, intimidation, threats and coercion aren't healthy communication strategies. Some situations require distance or professional support.

Try this: Try: Stop escalating, return at an agreed time when safe, acknowledge your part, and make a specific change.

Background reading: CDC · Violence prevention ↗

Friendship, solitude and loneliness＋

Friendships, communities and caring relationships provide different forms of support; solitude can be restorative.

Our vulnerability: Loneliness is a painful mismatch between connection wanted and connection experienced; being alone is not automatically loneliness.

Try this: Try: Choose the amount and kind of connection that fits you, rather than following someone else's quota.

Background reading: WHO · Social connection ↗

Different values, shared dignity＋

People can have different religions, politics, cultures, abilities and definitions of a good life.

Our vulnerability: Assuming everyone thinks like us blinds us; treating disagreement as proof of bad character destroys trust.

Try this: Try: Ask what harm, fear or value might underlie a disagreement without excusing harmful behavior.

Background reading: Annual Review of Psychology · Moral judgment ↗

HOME AND CARE

⌂ Families and generations

Families may be biological, chosen, blended, distant, complicated, joyful or painful. Caregiving is meaningful work, not an unlimited personal duty.

↑ Top
Caregiving without disappearing＋

Families and friends can share responsibilities, provide comfort and help one another through illness or aging.

Our vulnerability: One person doing everything can become exhausted; responsibility is often affected by money, availability and disability.

Try this: Try: Identify one task, an owner, a backup and a review date. Use our Family Handoff tool if useful.

Background reading: CDC · Protective factors in families ↗

Children need safety and belonging＋

Adults can model kindness, limits, curiosity and effective problem solving.

Our vulnerability: Fear, humiliation and violence may produce immediate compliance while undermining safety and trust.

Try this: Try: Explain a boundary briefly, offer an age-appropriate choice, and model how to repair a mistake.

Background reading: CDC · Protective factors in families ↗

Couples, households and shared labor＋

Shared routines and clear communication can help partners act as a team.

Our vulnerability: Unspoken assumptions about chores, money, intimacy or time can produce resentment.

Try this: Try: Each person names one task that feels heavy and one form of support they'd actually welcome.

Background reading: CDC · Preventing partner violence ↗

Generations, grief and change＋

Stories, rituals and ordinary happy moments connect generations; families adapt as roles change.

Our vulnerability: Old expectations, loss and unresolved conflict can make change difficult; not every relationship is safe or repairable.

Try this: Try: Preserve one meaningful memory or say something appreciative without demanding a particular response.

Background reading: CDC · Protective factors in families ↗

THE HUMAN WORLD

◎ Living together on Earth

Cooperation creates civilizations; unequal power, fear and misinformation can damage them. The conditions around us matter as much as individual effort.

↑ Top
Cooperation and the common good＋

Humans build institutions, share discoveries, volunteer, organize communities and help strangers.

Our vulnerability: Group loyalty can become exclusion. Systems built to protect can also be abused.

Try this: Try: Look for a practical benefit that includes people beyond your immediate group.

Background reading: Annual Review of Psychology · Moral judgment ↗

Hate, fear and the roots of harm＋

Protective instincts can help us respond to danger and stand up to injustice.

Our vulnerability: Fear and anger can harden into contempt, dehumanization, hatred, aggression or revenge; preventing harm takes individual, relationship and social action. Hatred is neither inevitable nor justified by difference.

Try this: Try: Before a high-stakes response, identify immediate safety, reliable information and safer alternatives.

Background reading: CDC · Violence prevention ↗

Fairness, scarcity and opportunity＋

Humans can share resources, design fairer rules and address unmet needs.

Our vulnerability: Not everyone starts with the same housing, education, healthcare, transport, income or safety.

Try this: Try: When someone struggles, consider both personal choices and the barriers around them.

Background reading: CDC · Social determinants of health ↗

Truth, bias and information＋

Curiosity and evidence let us challenge errors and improve collective decisions.

Our vulnerability: We are all vulnerable to selective evidence, emotionally compelling stories and misleading certainty.

Try this: Try: Check who made a claim, what evidence supports it and what would change your mind.

Background reading: Annual Review of Psychology · Moral judgment ↗

Power and responsibility＋

Leadership can organize care, protect rights and enable people to cooperate.

Our vulnerability: Power without accountability invites exploitation; 'for your own good' can become an excuse for control.

Try this: Try: Ask who benefits, who bears the risk, who can disagree and how the decision can be challenged.

Background reading: FTC · Recognizing manipulative design ↗

Our shared planet, different worlds＋

We inhabit one interconnected world and can act with care for other people, species and ecosystems.

Our vulnerability: People experience very different conditions and have different resources to respond to environmental challenges.

Try this: Try: Choose one practical act of stewardship that fits your circumstances; avoid shame or grand promises.

Background reading: CDC · Social determinants of health ↗

CURIOSITY AND DELIGHT

✦ Enjoy being alive

Art, play, music, science, humor, stories, nature and rest deserve a place in a meaningful life—even when nothing needs fixing.

↑ Top
Music, art and imagination＋

Rhythm, images, stories and performance help us express experience, build culture and connect.

Our vulnerability: Taste is personal. We don't need everyone to enjoy the same thing for it to be meaningful.

Try this: Try: Explore a song, artwork or tradition unlike your usual favorites; notice what you feel and wonder.

Background reading: BLS · American Time Use Survey 2025 ↗

Play, games and humor＋

Games and play let us experiment, connect, cooperate, compete and laugh without needing a practical goal.

Our vulnerability: Competition can become exclusion, and leisure can crowd out needs when its balance stops working for us.

Try this: Try: Make room for an enjoyable activity with no productivity requirement.

Background reading: BLS · American Time Use Survey 2025 ↗

Sports, movement and adventure＋

Movement, competition, outdoor experiences and skill learning create different kinds of enjoyment.

Our vulnerability: Risk is real; courage doesn't require ignoring limits, training, protective equipment or other people's safety.

Try this: Try: Choose a safe version of an activity that fascinates you and learn its basics.

Background reading: BLS · American Time Use Survey 2025 ↗

Science, nature and the unknown＋

Wonder pushes people to explore galaxies, oceans, living systems, engineering and the mind.

Our vulnerability: Not knowing can be uncomfortable, which makes confident but unsupported explanations tempting.

Try this: Try: Pick one surprising question and follow a reliable source—without requiring an instant conclusion.

Background reading: National Academies · How People Learn II ↗

Rest, comfort and ordinary happiness＋

Quiet mornings, pets, family meals, books, daydreaming and doing nothing useful can matter deeply.

Our vulnerability: Constant pressure to optimize life can make ordinary joys feel undeserved.

Try this: Try: Enjoy one small, harmless pleasure without needing to turn it into an achievement.

Background reading: BLS · American Time Use Survey 2025 ↗

OUR PROMISE, NOT OUR POWER

Learn. Choose. Respect. Repair.

The goal is not to make everyone think alike. It is to help each person develop understanding, agency, care and practical skills—while recognizing danger, unequal circumstances and the right to disagree. Kindness never requires surrendering personal safety.

Respect for self

Your limits, dignity, autonomy and well-being matter.

Respect for others

Other people have needs, agency and boundaries too.

Respect for everything

Consider how choices affect communities, living things and the shared world.

REAL LIFE IS COMPLICATED

Try a choice. See another perspective.

Open any response to explore a possible consequence. No right-answer score, no judgment, and no information saved. Context matters; urgent danger calls for safety first.

When anger rises

Someone repeats a claim you believe is wrong, and you feel yourself getting louder.

Explore possible responses (no score)
Keep explaining more forcefully

You might be heard less clearly as the other person becomes defensive. Repetition is not the same as understanding.

Pause, name the rising tension, and return to the issue

This can reduce immediate harm. Return when you said you would, and still address the underlying concern.

Pretend nothing happened

Silence may create a temporary break, but unspoken resentment can remain. A safe follow-up may help.

There is no universal script. Prioritize safety, respect, and an honest return to the issue.

When two people need different things

One partner wants conversation; the other needs quiet time.

Explore possible responses (no score)
Demand immediate attention

Urgency may feel like proof of caring, but it can override the other person's capacity.

Agree on a check-in time and protect the break

Both needs become visible. The agreement only works if the promised return is kept.

Withdraw indefinitely

Space can help, but uncertainty without communication may strain trust.

Consent, limits and predictability matter. If someone is controlling or threatening, seek safety rather than negotiation.

When caregiving falls on one person

A family member quietly handles most responsibilities and is becoming exhausted.

Explore possible responses (no score)
Assume they will ask if they need help

Sometimes they won't; fatigue and invisible work can go unnoticed.

Name tasks, owners, backups and review dates

Specific responsibilities make support more concrete. Consent and capacity still matter.

Tell them to be more grateful

That may increase guilt without changing the workload.

Shared care needs realistic roles, external support when possible, and permission to rest.

When a frightening story spreads

A shocking post claims a group is causing danger. Everyone is sharing it.

Explore possible responses (no score)
Share immediately to warn others

If the claim is false or incomplete, this can spread harm.

Check original evidence and independent sources first

Slowing down may prevent a mistake. Consider urgency, uncertainty and immediate safety.

Assume all reports are false

Blanket distrust can miss real risks. Evaluate claims rather than rejecting everything.

Curiosity should lead to verification, not humiliation or automatic certainty.

YOU ARE ALLOWED TO JUST BE

Not every visit needs a problem.

Read something surprising. Enjoy a new idea. Sit with a thought. Find a movie, walk outside, draw, laugh, make music, share a story—or do nothing useful for a while. Leisure and curiosity are part of human life, not rewards to be earned.
