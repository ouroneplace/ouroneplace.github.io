ONE PLACE · KNOWLEDGE ATLAS · FOUNDATION PREVIEW

A map of what we can learn together.

Knowledge should help people live, understand, care, and explore—not simply collect facts. Start with one question. Learn something useful here, practice a decision, and follow the evidence when you want to go further.

This is a beginning, not all of human knowledge. These six foundations are educational introductions with sources and limits. The Atlas does not replace healthcare, emergency services, community knowledge, libraries, science, or local expertise. More subjects and languages remain future work, not promises of existing coverage.

01 · HELP FIRST

When something matters right now

A reliable knowledge guide must say when a website is not enough. Real-world safety, people, and local responders matter more than finishing a lesson.

Why it matters

Urgent problems may require tools, direct observations, trusted people or local professionals; an online page cannot see what is happening around you.

Try these steps

  1. Check whether anyone is in immediate danger. Use your local emergency service when needed.
  2. Separate what you directly observed from what you only heard. Do not follow an unverified instruction that could increase danger.
  3. Choose one safe next action, then ask an appropriately trained person or local service if the risk is beyond your knowledge.

One-minute practice: Try naming one trusted way to contact local emergency or community help before you need it; rules and numbers differ worldwide.

Source to verify: WHO — Health literacy (external) ↗

Limit: General learning, not live crisis triage, first aid, diagnosis or local service availability.

02 · THE HUMAN BODY

Health information: understand before acting

Health literacy involves finding, understanding, judging and using information. It is not a test of how smart someone is.

Why it matters

A medical word or a surprising headline may hide what is known, what depends on your own history, and which questions need a clinician.

Try these steps

  1. Ask: What decision am I trying to make, and is it urgent?
  2. Find the original source or a trusted explanation. Check whether it applies to your age, conditions, medicines and location.
  3. Write down what you do not understand and the questions to ask a qualified care professional.

One-minute practice: Practice this question: “What are the benefits, risks, and alternatives for someone in my situation?”

Source to verify: WHO — Health literacy (external) ↗

Limit: No personalized treatment advice, medicine changes or guarantee of health outcomes.

03 · REASON AND EVIDENCE

How do we know a claim is true?

Confidence should grow from evidence, not from how loudly someone repeats a message or how convincing an image looks.

Why it matters

A picture, story, rumor or confident AI answer can be incomplete, mistaken or misleading. Good reasoning protects independent decisions.

Try these steps

  1. State the exact claim in simple words. Is it a fact, an interpretation, a prediction or an opinion?
  2. Find the original source and inspect its date, method, actual findings and possible limitations.
  3. Compare with independent, competent sources. Say “I don't know yet” when the evidence is missing or disputed.

One-minute practice: Ask of a surprising post: “What evidence would change my mind, and where would I look for it?”

Source to verify: UNESCO — Open Science (external) ↗

Limit: Not every question has a single measurable answer; distinguish empirical facts from ethical or cultural judgments.

04 · OUR SHARED EARTH

People depend on living systems

Our lives depend on water, soil, air, climate and other living things. Protecting ecosystems can also strengthen human well-being.

Why it matters

A short-term fix in one place may shift harm to another community or species. Local conditions and ecological tradeoffs matter.

Try these steps

  1. Name one part of your local environment that supports life: drinking water, healthy soil, shade, pollinators or clean air.
  2. Trace one connection: where it comes from, who depends on it, and what threatens it.
  3. Choose a responsible action that is safe and locally appropriate; verify before altering habitats, handling wildlife or planting nonnative species.

One-minute practice: Ask: “What lives here, what does it need, and how can we avoid unnecessary harm?”

Source to verify: UNEP — Nature-based solutions (external) ↗

Limit: This is a foundation, not an environmental impact assessment or a claim that every local action works everywhere.

05 · LIVING WITH OTHERS

Respect, listen and repair

People have different histories, abilities, cultures, responsibilities and views. Understanding someone is not the same as agreeing with every action.

Why it matters

Better decisions consider ourselves, the people affected, and the wider consequences. Respect also includes boundaries and safety.

Try these steps

  1. Describe what actually happened before deciding another person's intention.
  2. Ask what the other person needs and whether they want a conversation, space or practical help.
  3. Explain your own needs without threats or coercion. Seek appropriate outside support when there is abuse or immediate danger.

One-minute practice: Try: “I noticed ____. How do you see it? I need ____. What could work for both of us?”

Source to verify: IFLA–UNESCO — Public Library Manifesto (external) ↗

Limit: This lesson offers a respectful approach, not a claim that every relationship can be repaired or a substitute for abuse support.

06 · THE UNIVERSE

Earth is one world among many

The Sun is a star. Our solar system has eight recognized planets, including Earth, and many smaller objects. Astronomy uses observations to test explanations.

Why it matters

Learning about space shows how much humans can discover—and how much remains unknown. A stunning photo alone does not establish an extraordinary claim.

Try these steps

  1. Distinguish an observation (a point of light moved) from an interpretation (it must be a spacecraft).
  2. Ask how astronomers measured a claim, what instruments were used and what other explanations fit.
  3. Use reliable astronomy sources to check what is established and what is still being studied.

One-minute practice: Name the Sun as a star, Earth as the third planet from it, and one question you want to investigate.

Source to verify: NASA — Solar System Facts (external) ↗

Limit: We have strong evidence for our solar system; we have not established all possibilities for life elsewhere.

LEARN TO CHECK CLAIMS

Try a claim before you believe it.

These stories are fictional. The goal is not a score. Practice finding original evidence and making safer choices when information is incomplete. You can read every explanation without selecting an answer.

A dramatic health claim appears online

A fictional post says a special drink cures every illness. It cites no study and urges people to stop other care.

Which response best protects independent judgment?

Read the explanation without answering

Extraordinary health claims require sound evidence. Do not stop prescribed care on the basis of an unverified post. Ask a qualified professional before making personal treatment decisions.

A striking sky photo goes viral

Someone shares a blurry image and says it proves a visitor from another planet landed nearby.

What can the image alone establish?

Read the explanation without answering

An image is an observation to investigate, not proof of one explanation. Ask for date, source, context, original data and independent confirmation.

A worrying local alert is forwarded

A message claims a town's water should not be used today. The sender cannot identify the water utility or a dated official notice.

What is the best next step?

Read the explanation without answering

Safety alerts can be real and urgent, but context and geography matter. Confirm with the responsible local water provider or public-health authority through a known, independent channel, and seek an alternative safe supply if a credible current advisory applies.

The practice does not collect your answers, save them, contact anyone or decide what is true about a real event.
